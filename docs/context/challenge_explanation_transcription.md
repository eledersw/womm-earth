NASA has Earth science data for a lot of different phenomena. Scientists (and you) can analyze the data to see if there are trends. There may be trends in the temperature of a certain region or the amount of cloud cover or the amount of solar radiation a region receives. Trends can be found where snow falls, the depth of the snow, and the amount of water in the snow.

The oceans can have trends, too. The temperature of the ocean surface, [music] the height of the ocean surface, and the color of the ocean water, which is affected both by living organisms and particles in the water.
NASA missions and projects look at how much water is being carried by rivers, how much water is stored underground, and how much water is found in the soil. This is very important for water resources, the water that people use every day, and for agriculture, the water needed to grow crops. NASA has a lot of data about the weather, how much rain is falling in a particular area, the wind speeds, the atmospheric pressure, and the water vapor that can become a powerful storm. Data can even be used to look at the timing of the seasons and if the temperature of a season is changing. It's also possible to detect trends occurring in a particular season, which might be a little harder, but it can be done. 

Now, let's try an example. In several summers, including the summer of 2026, there have been large wildfires in central Canada. The smoke from these wildfires has been transported long distances and has affected air quality in the United States. For this example, we will use aerosol optical depth, AOD, a variable that indicates how much the transmission of light is reduced by the presence of substances in the air like the particles that compose smoke. Now we will make a time series of AOD using data from the moderate resolution imaging spectral radiometer modus on the Aqua satellite. This important instrument began observing the Earth in 2002. The time series will average the value of AOD over the entire state of Wisconsin for every month since the observations began. There might be a trend: the average value of AOD over Wisconsin may be increasing in the summer months. There is much less fire and smoke in the winter months.

Significance for science means using a statistical test to determine if the data in the time series meet the statistical standard for significance. The simplest way to do this for a time series is to calculate the p value. If the p value is less than 0.05, the time series meets the standard for significance, it is called a significant trend. If the p value is more than 0.05, the time series does not meet the standard for significance. That doesn't mean it isn't a trend or that it isn't important. Sometimes all that is needed to make a trend significant is a longer time series. 

Here are the steps to follow to be an Earth science data trend detective with NASA data. Your app should be able to do this: 
- One, decide on the data variable or variables for analysis.
- Two, choose a region for the analysis and that can be the entire world.
- Three, select the time period for the time series.
- Four, create the time series and examine it. Does it appear to have a trend?
- Five, analyze the trend for significance.

Here are two things to note. NASA has a lot of data. Your app can work on one particular type of data such as rainfall or soil moisture or it can be capable of choosing different kinds of data. Sometimes choosing the data is more complicated than doing an analysis. Also, some NASA data sets have data points every hour or every 3 hours. It is easier to determine trends over time for data points that are weekly or monthly.
